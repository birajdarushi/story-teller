Title: The Adventure of the Veiled Lodger
Arthur Conan Doyle

When one considers that Mr. Sherlock Holmes was in active practice for twenty-three years, and that during seventeen of these I was allowed to cooperate with him and to keep notes of his doings, it will be clear that I have a mass of material at my command. The problem has always been not to find but to choose. There is the long row of year-books which fill a shelf, and there are the dispatch-cases filled with documents, a perfect quarry for the student not only of crime but of the social and official scandals of the late Victorian era.

It is not reasonable to suppose that every one of these cases gave Holmes the opportunity of showing those curious gifts of instinct and observation which I have endeavoured to set forth in these memoirs. Sometimes he had with much effort to pick the fruit, sometimes it fell easily into his lap. But the most terrible human tragedies were often involved in those cases which brought him the fewest personal opportunities, and it is one of these which I now desire to record.

One forenoon—it was late in 1896—I received a hurried note from Holmes asking for my attendance. When I arrived I found him seated in a smoke-laden atmosphere, with an elderly, motherly woman of the buxom landlady type in the corresponding chair in front of him.

“This is Mrs. Merrilow, of South Brixton,” said my friend. “Mrs. Merrilow has an interesting story to tell.”

Mrs. Merrilow explained that her lodger, Mrs. Ronder, had lived with her for seven years and never revealed her face, which was horribly mutilated. Recently, her health had declined, and she cried out in distress during the night, speaking of “murder” and calling someone a “monster.” She wished to confess the truth before dying.

Holmes recalled the Abbas Parva tragedy involving Ronder’s traveling wild beast show. Ronder, a cruel and violent man, was killed during an apparent accident involving a lion named Sahara King. His wife was horribly injured, her face torn apart.

Holmes suspected inconsistencies in the case. The lion had escaped, killed Ronder, and attacked Mrs. Ronder, but certain details did not add up—especially reports of another man’s voice during the incident.

Holmes and Watson visited Mrs. Ronder. She revealed the truth:

She had lived a miserable life with her abusive husband, Ronder. He beat her and terrorized everyone in the circus. She fell in love with Leonardo, the strongman. Together, they plotted Ronder’s murder.

Leonardo created a weapon—a club with nails arranged like a lion’s claws—to make the death appear as though caused by the animal. One night, as Ronder and his wife went to feed the lion, Leonardo struck and killed him.

Mrs. Ronder then released the lion to complete the illusion.

However, the plan went terribly wrong. The lion, sensing blood, attacked her. Leonardo lost his nerve and fled instead of saving her. The lion mauled her face, leaving her disfigured for life.

She chose to protect Leonardo, lying to authorities. He later died in an accident.

Now, near death, she confessed everything to Holmes—not for justice, but for peace of mind.

Holmes, moved by her suffering, urged her not to take her own life, suggesting that enduring suffering could still hold meaning.

Days later, Holmes received a small bottle of poison—prussic acid—with a note:
“I send you my temptation. I will follow your advice.”

Holmes believed she chose to live on.

End.
